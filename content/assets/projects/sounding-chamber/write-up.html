Sounding Chamber

Every move is a find-and-replace across an entire language.

A puzzle game where the failure you cannot see is the one that matters: a board that is still playable and can no longer be won. Nothing is generated until exhaustive search has proved otherwise. The write-up below is mostly about the things that check turned out to be wrong about.

Level 1 · The Bear Necessity

year 1200 · 6 left

“The king shall be slain by a bear.”

Spelling (frozen)Sound

Play it. Merge bear into beer in six generations without ever letting pen meet pin. Each card shows how many words it would move before you commit.

One disclosure, because a later section depends on it: this engine works on letters, not sounds. The slashes hold spelling standing in for pronunciation. That shortcut is exactly what the study further down is about.

What it is

A prophecy binds to sounds, not meanings

You cannot break the prophecy. You can, over six generations, change how the word bear is pronounced until it is the word beer, at which point the prophecy is satisfied by a falling barrel and the king survives. In the year 1250, a barrel falls from the brewer's cart. The king, regrettably, is slain by a beer. He gets better.

The catch is that pronunciation change is not surgical. A sound change applies to every word in the language at once. Aim at bear and you also hit bread, pen, and ale. If pen and pin ever become the same word, the royal scribe orders a thousand pins, the army receives a thousand pens, and the siege does not go well. The puzzle is not the target. The puzzle is the collateral damage.

This is also real linguistics. Say Mary, marry and merry aloud. For most of North America those are now one word, three vowels collapsed into one before an r, and almost nobody who did it noticed. The pen and pin merger already finished across most of the American South, generations ago. The frightening part of a merger is not that it happens. It is that it is invisible from the inside, to the people it happens to.

Five levels exist, each built around one real mechanism. The Brewer's Flood: the queen must drown in a flood, so make the flood a flute, but hardening the final consonant first destroys the environment the rounding rule needs. The Chain Gang: the champion is felled by a dart, and dirt is two rungs up a vowel ladder that moves all at once. The Loan Shark: a word arrives from abroad mid-game, immune to every rule you played before it docked.

Six words, before they are needed

The vocabulary this page runs on

merger
A sound change that makes two previously different words sound the same. Permanent, and it takes the whole vocabulary with it.
e → i / _r
Standard notation for a sound change. Read it as: e becomes i, but only when an r comes next. Everything after the slash is the condition. Without a slash the rule fires everywhere, which is usually the trap.
feeding, bleeding
One rule creating the conditions another rule needs, or destroying them. This is why order matters, and why a card you need can be the card that kills you.
bricked
The run can continue but can no longer be won. Nothing on screen says so, which is why a solver has to.
par
The fewest moves in which a level can be won, established by exhaustive search rather than by the designer's guess.
IPA
The International Phonetic Alphabet. One symbol per sound, so /dˈoːɹ/ describes door without English spelling getting a vote.

The engineering

The design document is the test suite

The engine is pure functions over token arrays. A word is a list of tokens; a rule is a function from that list to another list; a level is data. Nothing is random once play starts, and two words are the same word when their lists join to the same string.

That buys one thing worth having: the prose playthroughs in the design document can be run. The story that says the unconditioned raising loses immediately on pen and pin, and also strands the target out of reach exists as three assertions with those exact values. There is no build. That is the measurement, not a boast. The prototype is four HTML pages and four JavaScript files served by a small Python static server. Timing a build step that does not exist is the honest version of a fast build, and it says nothing about what a real one would cost.

Verification

Nothing is generated until search says it can be won

Two failure modes matter more than losing. A level can be bricked, and a generated level can be impossible. Both are invisible to playtesting and both are trivially detectable by search, so a breadth-first solver walks every legal sequence of card plays up to the turn budget and returns the shortest win, the shortest win with the bonus, or nothing at all.

The space is small because a hand is five or six cards and a budget is six turns, and it is small by design: the levels were built shallow enough that verification is total rather than sampled. That is a trade, not a limitation. Depth was spent on ordering constraints instead of branching factor.

What the search is actually for

Across 5 authored levels and 500 generated ones, brute force never found a shorter solution than the one already written down. Par and the author's own line agreed 505 times out of 505. As a way of discovering optimal play, the solver produced nothing.

Its value is entirely in the negative direction: proving a state has no win. That is the check a designer cannot do by hand and a playtester cannot do at all, and it is the only thing that catches bricking. Verification earns its cost when the counterexample is the product.

What this does not show The five authored pars were almost certainly read off this same solver when the levels were written, so "agree" on those five is a consistency check, not independent confirmation. The 500 generated levels are the real evidence, and they are drawn from only three hand-written puzzle families, so this cannot distinguish "the solver finds nothing extra" from "there is nothing extra to find in a puzzle of this shape."

Determinism

The same seed is the same Tuesday

The daily puzzle is generated from a hash of the date string, so every player gets the same board without a server deciding anything. That matters for a reason beyond tidiness: if the generator drifted between processes, the verification that ran when the puzzle was built would be describing a different puzzle than the one played, and two players comparing scores would be comparing different games.

The three families are the three shapes a daily puzzle can take, and an uneven draw would mean the daily has a recognisable silhouette that a regular player would learn to read off the board. A chi-square test against a uniform draw gives 0.68 on two degrees of freedom, well under the 5.99 that would flag a skew.

What this does not show No detectable bias at n=500, which is not the same as no bias. A real skew of a couple of points would pass this test unnoticed. Three families is also a small alphabet, and this says nothing about bias in the words, onsets or prophecy text drawn inside a family, which is where a player would actually notice repetition first.

The safety net that has never caught anything

The generator is written to resample. It draws a candidate, rejects it if two words are born identical, rejects it if the canonical solution does not win, rejects it if search cannot confirm solvability, and retries up to twenty times. In 500 draws it was never used. Every level was accepted on the first attempt.

Zero rejections in 500 trials puts a 95% upper bound of about 0.6% on the pooled rejection rate. So the retry loop is either dead weight or an untested branch guarding a rare event, and an untested branch in the one code path that decides what a player is allowed to see is the worse of the two readings. It should be exercised on purpose, with a deliberately broken family, rather than left to chance.

What this does not show That bound is pooled across all three families. Each family only got about 167 draws, so the per-family bound is roughly 1.8%, three times looser: one pathological family could be rejecting at a percent or two and hide inside the pooled figure. A fourth family written next month could reject constantly and none of this would have predicted it.

The thing that broke

The display agreed with itself and was wrong

A second prototype speaks the drifting words aloud, so you can hear a merger happen. It highlights which words in an overheard line a rule will affect. It picked those words by looking at their spelling.

A card demonstrating the fronting of the uː vowel highlighted the word door, because door contains the letters “oo.” It does not contain the sound. Its pronunciation is /dˈoːɹ/, a different vowel entirely, inherited from a different history. The rule ran, the display lit up, and the audio was identical before and after, because nothing had happened.

This is the game's own premise turned against it. English spelling lies about English sound; that lie is why the prophecy loophole works at all, and it is why a tool that reads spelling will confidently report changes that no ear could hear. The fix was to select words by running the rule against their actual phonemes. To size what that changed, I ran both selectors over the same 139-word vocabulary and the five rules the tool can demonstrate.

97 word-rule pairs were flagged by at least one method, and the two disagreed on 33 of them. The two directions are different failures and should not be pooled into one rate: in 25 cases spelling fires and no sound moves, and in 8 cases spelling misses a change that does happen. Against spelling's own 89 flags, 25 are spurious.

The worst offender is r-dropping. Of 32 words whose spelling has an r after a vowel, 18 move no phoneme at all, because brother, feather and hammer end in a single r-coloured vowel rather than a vowel followed by an r.

What this does not show An earlier draft of this section put a 95% confidence interval on that 34%. That was wrong and I have removed it. These 695 decisions are a census of a fixed word list run through two deterministic programs, not a sample of anything: run it again and you get 33 of 97 with probability one. There is no sampling distribution for an interval to describe. The clustering makes it worse still, since 18 of the 33 disagreements come from one transcription convention rather than 18 independent events.

What the number really depends on is the transcriber. This phonemiser writes brother as /bɹˈʌðɚ/, one r-coloured vowel; a transcriber who wrote it as ə followed by ɹ would make r-dropping fire and most of those 18 would vanish. The word list is also the tool's own pool, chosen partly because those words illustrate these rules, which pushes the flagged share up relative to running text. And the two selectors were compared to each other, never to a human phonetician. No ground truth was consulted, so none of the 33 is established as an error. They are disagreements.

The metric that lied

A passing check that was measuring the wrong thing

The speech package lives in one place and is copied into each consumer that needs it. A sync script stamps a content hash for every copied file, and a check flag re-reads them so a copy that has drifted from its source gets caught. It reports clean, at version 0.2.0.

The version is not 0.2.0. The package was bumped by editing a version file, and the constant compiled into the module, the one any consumer reads at runtime, was left behind at 0.1.0. The hash check compares each copy against its source and both are equally stale, so they match perfectly and the light goes green. It verifies that the copies agree. It never asks whether they are right.

node scrap_scripts/js/15_version_invariant.test.mjs

PASS  VERSION file matches the sync stamp        "0.2.0" == "0.2.0"
FAIL  sc-tts.js constant matches VERSION         expected "0.2.0", got "0.1.0"
FAIL  vendored copy constant matches VERSION     expected "0.2.0", got "0.1.0"

1/3 passed                                                          exit 1

Writing that test was the diagnosis, not the fix, and stopping there would have reproduced the original failure one level up: a correct check nobody runs. The structural repair is to make the drift unrepresentable by deriving the module's constant from the version file at load time, so there is one source and nothing to disagree. Detection is what you build when prevention is not available; here it was available.

The number that does not mean what it says

“100 game-ready rules,” none of which the game can run

Content is the expensive part, so there is a database of 181 real sound changes with citations, environments and trap ratings. A hundred of them carry a field holding the rule rewritten in the token syntax the puzzle engine uses. The browser counts those and displays “100 game-ready.”

That field is read by exactly one thing, and it is not the game. Five reads, all inside the database browser: render it, export it, filter on it, count it. The engine's rules are 23 hand-written functions and it has no parser for the syntax, so the number of database rules playable today is zero. “Game-ready” describes the shape of a string, not a capability. Written honestly it reads: 100 rules transcribed into a syntax nothing parses yet.

What this does not show The transcription is lossy in a way the count also hides. The pen–pin merger conditions on three nasals; the transcribed version covers two, silently dropping ŋ. A parser would make 100 rules loadable without making them faithful, and I have not measured how many of the 100 lose something on the way in.

An assumption, audited

The cost that never came down

Turning a word into phonemes runs a speech engine compiled to WebAssembly. That engine is a command-line program, so the wrapper starts a fresh instance for every word, and the note I wrote at the time says this is “fine for a bench; flagged as a perf item if it ever feels sluggish.” Assumptions like that usually survive on an unstated belief that repetition gets cheaper. I went back and measured my own note.

40 consecutive calls, ten words cycled four times. If instantiation were being amortised, later calls would be faster than earlier ones.
WindownMean msSD
Calls 1–1010147.79.3
Calls 11–3020156.24.7
Calls 31–4010151.43.3
All 4040152.96.8

No amortisation. The last ten calls minus the first ten is +3.7 ms with a standard error of 3.1, a 95% interval of −2.4 to +9.8, so the difference is indistinguishable from noise in either direction. The cost is one whole WebAssembly instantiation per word and it is paid again every time. Phonemising the 139-word vocabulary for the study above took 20.3 seconds of wall clock to produce a few hundred bytes of transcription. For a tool that speaks one line at a time that is genuinely fine. For a game that wants to voice a lexicon as it drifts, it is a redesign, and my note understated it.

What this does not show An earlier draft of this section claimed the last ten calls were slower than the first ten. They were not, measurably; the interval above straddles zero and I have replaced the claim with the interval. The middle twenty calls are in fact the slowest window of the three, which no story about warm-up predicts and which 40 samples cannot explain.

Measured in Node on one machine, not in the browser where this actually runs, where the compiled module is cached across instantiations and absolute times would likely be lower. What transfers is the shape, not the number. One voice, one narrow word-length band.

Where it actually is

What is not built

This is a JavaScript reference implementation with a solver, a verified generator, a 181-rule content database and a working speech path. It is not a game anyone has played.

  • The repository is local. Nothing on this page can be audited by a reader. Publishing it is the first item on the list and its absence is the fairest criticism anyone can make here.
  • The port has not started. The target engine is Godot, a free game engine that would let this ship as a real downloadable build. Its project directory contains one empty placeholder file. This is the largest remaining piece.
  • No player has touched it. Every claim on this page is about internal consistency, not about whether the puzzles are any good.
  • The database is not wired to the engine, as above.
  • The phoneme translation table is incomplete. When a second voice backend went in, all five demonstrable rules mapped with nothing unmapped, and I recorded that as coverage. Against every symbol the 181-rule database actually uses it covers 23 of 39, and 27 rules touch a symbol with no entry. Five rules was the sample.
  • The speech engine is GPL-3.0, so shipping its binary in a public build pulls those obligations onto the build. That gate is logged and unresolved; the binary is deliberately not committed.
  • The pitch document says “29 assertions green.” It is 41. The figure was written before a second assertion file existed and nobody updated it, which is the same drift the version check above also missed.
