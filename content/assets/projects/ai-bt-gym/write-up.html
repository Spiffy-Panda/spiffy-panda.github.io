Plain language first

What this actually is

A behavior tree is game AI written as a flowchart. A root node asks its children, in order, whether they want to act this frame. Am I hurt? Then heal. Is the enemy close? Then punch. Otherwise, walk toward them. The first branch that succeeds wins the frame, and the rest never run.

The useful part is that the whole strategy is a file, not code. It saves as JSON, which means a language model can be handed one, shown where it lost, and asked to rewrite the branch responsible. Gym here means the place that answers the next question: was the rewrite actually better?

Fighters play a full round-robin, everyone against everyone, and get rated with the same maths chess uses. On that scale a 100-point gap means the stronger side wins about 64 percent of the time, so it is a real gap but not a wall. The rest of this page is what happened when that loop ran for eight generations.

One more word worth unpacking. Deterministic means the same starting seed always produces the byte-identical fight. Not similar. Identical. That property is what turns a replay into a test, and it is the thing most of this project's engineering protects.

That is the entire representation. Nine node types, conditions as expression strings, actions as names the world knows how to run. A model reads and writes this directly.

The worked example

Cyan lost thirty-five matches out of thirty-five

This is the whole loop on one fighter, with the artifact from each stage. It is also the generation where most of the field got worse, which is the more useful half of the result.

Stage one, the symptom

Cyan_ZoneController finished generation two on a 0–35 record at 717 rating, the worst in the project's history. Its predecessor generations were 2–33 and 3–32. Losing is not itself informative. The logs had to say why.

Stage two, the diagnosis

Action counts are recorded per match. Cyan issued 442 retreat actions per fight. Red, the next worst, issued 183. Cyan was not being outfought. It was running away from every engagement it started, then losing on the clock and on chip damage.

Stage three, the rewrite

Retreat was removed everywhere except inside 80 units. Anchor locking dropped to a chain length of 90 so it would create swing points more often. Zone shots now fire from the anchor instead of from a safe distance. Dive release was added on descent above 50 units per second.

Cyan_ZoneController rating, generations 0 to 7

Record went 0–35 to 22–13. The gain then decayed over four generations as the rest of the field adapted, ending 200 points below the peak.

Why the prompt looks like that

The tree alone is not enough context and the raw replay is far too much. What works is the tree plus roughly seven representative matches, each compressed to counts rather than events: how often each action fired, damage split across early, middle and late phases, grapple attach counts and durations, and where on the map it spent its time.

Asking for one concrete change rather than a rewrite is deliberate. An unconstrained rewrite changes many things at once and the next tournament cannot tell you which one mattered. Generation three is the cautionary case: several fighters got more than one edit, five of six regressed, and the per-fighter cause is still ambiguous.

The loop was driven by Claude Opus 4.6, with the diagnosis, the tree edits and the written change log all produced from these prompts. Every generation's reasoning is committed alongside the trees it produced, so a claim like grapple volume is the meta can be checked against the tournament that followed it.

The two rows that teach the most are the boring ones

Wall awareness is worth nothing. Five wins, four losses, one draw, and average scores identical to the point. The subtree runs, the fighters break the wall, and it does not change who wins. That is a feature built and then measured into the bin.

Hazard awareness on the hazard map wins every game but only by four points of score, while the same subtree on the combined map wins by 116. The knowledge is nearly worthless alone and decisive in combination, which is not what anyone predicted going in.

The season that broke

Half of all matches ended in a draw

An earlier Beacon Brawl season posted records like 15 wins, 0 losses, 30 draws. The cause was geometry, and the behaviour logs had been saying so for three generations.

Draw rate by generation

225 matches per generation. Average match length fell from 1,901 ticks to 1,277 in the same step, because matches started ending on score instead of the clock.

What the behaviour logs were saying

The centre beacon is worth double and sits on a floating platform. The platform was placed higher than a jump plus a grapple hook could carry a pawn, so nobody could ever take it. Both teams held one side beacon each, neither could break the tie, and the clock ran out. Over half of every season.

The evidence was in the logs the whole time. Across 360 recorded team-matches, all four teams spent 91 to 92 percent of the match standing on the ground and 8 to 9 percent on the platform. Movement accounted for roughly 99 percent of every action issued. Shooting a pistol was 0.8 percent. Firing the rifle was 0.5 percent. Using the hook at all was 0.4 percent.

Nobody reads that as a level-design bug until they go looking. The win-loss record hides it completely, because a symmetric stalemate looks like a balanced game.

The engineering that makes the rest possible

A replay that disagrees with the game is a failing test

Every claim above depends on a run being repeatable. Three mechanisms enforce that, and one of them catches its own bugs.

Fixed step, no engine in the loop

Physics, combat and tree evaluation are plain C# at a fixed 60 Hz. No frame timing, no engine calls in the hot path. The renderer only ever consumes a recording.

Every result carries its seed and a build hash

Each generation writes its match seed and a SHA-256 of the compiled simulation. Change the physics and the hash changes, which marks earlier standings as belonging to a different game rather than a comparable one.

Playback re-runs the real simulation

A replay stores a checkpoint every ten ticks plus the seed and both trees. The viewer re-simulates and compares against the checkpoints as it plays, so it can only display a pass when recording and live physics still agree.

Seven bugs found, and only two were in the game

The other five were in recording. Damage, hook grabs and parries all worked in the simulation and all logged as zero. That is the worse failure of the two, because a silent metric turns the whole rewrite loop into guesswork. Checkpoint replay is what settled it, by showing kills accumulating from shots the counter insisted had never landed, at a rate of about one kill per thirty-three shots.

The numbers a second engineer would ask for

Build takes 4.7 seconds. The full suite above runs 150 evaluation matches in 59 seconds on one desktop core, engine startup included. Each match is up to 90 simulated seconds, so that is roughly 230 times real time, single threaded.

The failing test is left failing on purpose. Swing traversal reaches 215 pixels against a 500 pixel target, which means chained pendulum movement does not yet work the way the design assumes. It is a real gap in the movement model, and hiding it would remove the only signal that it exists.

The honest gap is automation. Nothing runs this on commit. The build hash and the checkpoint comparison are both capable of gating a merge, and neither is wired to one. That is the next piece of work, not a solved problem.

Two arenas

The same runtime in two different games

Both hand the tree an identical interface: resolve a named variable, run a named action, report the tick. That three-method contract is the entire coupling between strategy and world.

Dueling Fighters

1 v 1

A side-on brawl whose only verb is a fist launched on a chain. The chain can extend, lock at length, stick to a surface and reel in. Punching, wall-jumping, swinging and hammering are all the same mechanic held in different states, which is why a tree can invent tactics without new actions being written for it.

2 v 2 · zones

Two roles per team. The Grappler keeps the chain and adds a point-blank grab that leaves its target taking 50 percent extra damage for a second. Leaves

Ask the world, or tell it to act.

  • Condition
  • Action

Conditions are stored as expression strings, which suits a model writing JSON and does not suit anyone writing trees by hand. A specification layer resolves that: typed variable references build the identical string when the tree is constructed, so hand-authored trees get autocomplete and the per-tick path still evaluates a plain string. The variable set covers self, opponent, arena geometry, volatile state such as standing in a hazard, and objective state such as capture progress.

Limits

What this does not show

Stated plainly, because most of these are visible in the data above if you look for them.

Thirty matches is not enough to rank four teams

In the current Beacon Brawl series the rank order changes almost every generation and the whole field sits inside 60 rating points. One team went 1099, 1102, 1169, 1011, 906, 972 across six generations without its tree changing character. That is churn, not learning, and the sample is too small to claim otherwise.

The rewrite loop is one model and one author

There is no baseline to compare it against. Nobody ran random mutation, or a second model, or a human-only control on the same generations, so the honest claim is that targeted rewrites moved one fighter 350 points, not that this method beats alternatives.

Generation three regressed five fighters out of six

Multiple edits per fighter in a single generation makes attribution impossible. The fix is one change per fighter per generation, which costs more tournament runs and has not been done yet.

Determinism is verified on one machine

The build hash guards against changed physics, not against a different floating-point environment. Cross-machine determinism is untested, and nothing runs the checkpoint comparison automatically on commit.
