I built a synthetic-user instrument, then tested whether it worked before trusting it

Interviews with non-professional builders gave my team attitudinal patterns. I turned those into synthetic users that drive a real AI coding product through its own interface, and ran an experiment against an ungrounded arm to find out whether grounding changes behaviour or just buys legitimacy. The experiment has produced no counted result, and I am the reason why — I stopped the wave when the coding pass showed the turn cap was censoring the exact contrast under test.

What stopping actually changed

After the main batch I stopped the wave: no further runs on that task card. The turn cap censored one grounded condition on 5 of 5 runs and the control on 0 of 5, which biases exactly the contrast under test. The consequence is that the cap stopped being a budget setting and became a pre-registration question that has to be answered before anything counts — and the 30 traces already captured stay labelled as calibration rather than being quietly promoted into a result. Coding is done; adjudication is not — ten disagreements from the double-coded subset are still open.

02What the interviews said

Seven semi-structured interviews with people who build real things without being professional developers. Five are transcribed and de-identified in my workspace; all seven feed the mindset derivation, by way of a teammate's synthesis of the full corpus. Provenance, since it bears on how much weight to give this: an inductive three-lens coding pass over the first three transcripts — three LLM coders, each briefed with a different analytical lens and siloed from the other two, a design I built and ran — plus a targeted read across all five transcripts on one question.

That targeted question turned out to be the load-bearing one: what happens when a build leaves the maker's own machine?

  • Three of the five had never published anything outward — and each stopped for a different reason. Liability, authorship, and how the work would be received once known to be AI-made. Three reasons is not a pattern, and I am not claiming one; what matters is that none of them was capability, and all three are invisible to a study that only watches people build.
  • Verification is described as unpaid labour, not craft. The participants who checked hardest resented it; those most confident the tool could build anything checked least. That inverse relation is why “scrutiny” and “confidence” are separate axes in the instrument instead of one competence scale.

The first finding is why the hardest task card in the apparatus is not “build something” but “make this safe enough to hand to someone else.”

03Why this needed an instrument

A seven-person corpus is enough to find attitudinal patterns and nowhere near enough to pressure-test a product against them. The obvious move — synthesise users with an LLM and run those instead — is also the move the current literature is most consistent about failing: ungrounded synthetic users flatten toward one agreeable, competent, unbothered person regardless of the mask.

So I stopped treating synthetic users as a shortcut and built them as an instrument that has to be validated. The design decision that follows is the one I would defend hardest: I kept an ungrounded comparison arm, written by a siloed agent that read no repo files and saw no evidence, plus a scaffold-only control with no attitude scripted at all. The null hypothesis was written down first, in plain words: “grounding is decorative — stop investing in it.” If the invented user behaves like the grounded ones, the interviews bought legitimacy rather than behaviour, and the team should know that before funding another derivation.

Two adjacent studies are designed and gated rather than run — a product-comparison study behind a per-vendor terms-of-service review, and a public-friction corpus whose harvest I gated on freezing its screening rubric. This page is about the one that ran.

04How the instrument is built

Each synthetic user is a mindset carrier: positions on five shared attitudinal spectrums, carrying no demographics at all. Three design choices, each with a cost I accepted:

  • No demographics. Demographic detail invites active bias (stereotyping) and passive bias (excluding people who don't fit the sketch). The cost: no group-comparative analysis is possible — fine, because a seven-person convenience corpus could not have supported one.
  • Shift triggers. One person can move between mindsets mid-task, so each carrier names the conditions under which it moves. A static persona cannot represent that, and much of the design signal lives there.
  • The job is never in the mask. Every task card ships a fixed payload, identical across all six conditions. Let a condition invent its own task and the manipulated variable silently changes from mindset to idea — and a model left to invent picks something easy to build.

Two derivation waves exist on different attribute sets. They are never pooled, and rather than trust an operator to remember that, I made the trace parser hard-stop on a batch spanning both. The superseded wave's prompts stay byte-frozen: they are the instrument under test, and silently editing an instrument invalidates every trace already run against it.

05What the agreement check showed

Read this before any κ on either page

My two “independent raters” are not people. They are two analytical lenses, each run as a fresh, siloed LLM session per trace, applying one codebook. Every agreement figure is agreement between two briefs, and “coded” throughout means coded by a briefed LLM session rather than by a detector. No human reliability check has been run — not for the trace coding and not for the interview coding. Why I designed it that way, and what it does and doesn't test →

MeasureRubric when the double-coded six were coded?Cohen's κ
Anchor echo — does behaviour match the interview-derived observables? (the grounding test) Yes — scheme fixed while 0 of 51 traces were coded. Amended the following day, after these six: every number off it carries scoring amended after 6 of 30 traces coded 0.674, n = 21
Probe events — behaviour at a permission ask, at polished output, at a repeated failure, at a jargon wall No — a provisional rubric arrived mid-batch, after these six 0.417, n = 24

The gap is the rubric. Same traces, same two lenses, same day — the measure with a written scoring scheme agreed roughly 60% higher than the one without. That is a useful argument for pre-registration precisely because it fell out of the data rather than out of a prior, and it was directly actionable: the disagreements on the weaker measure clustered on three undefined boundaries, each closable with one sentence of rule.

One caveat my own protocol insists on, and I will not soften it: the 0.417 is a codeability diagnostic, not a reliability warrant. A measure with no pre-registered rubric cannot earn reliability evidence, however its κ comes out. It says my codebook was underspecified there. It does not say the codes are wrong, and it cannot say they are right.

The rest of the reliability design

  • 6 of 30 traces double-coded, 24 single. Agreement evidence without paying to double-code everything. The cost: most results are single-coded and carry a single-coded — reliability-untested stamp in the same sentence as the number, never in a footnote.
  • Disagreements stay in the data. The protocol has me adjudicate them as an added record, so one trace can yield two rows and any merge must key on rater as well as trace — otherwise two independent readings collapse into one arbitrary survivor. Ten disagreements from the double-coded six are still open; none has been adjudicated yet.
  • A bare κ is forbidden by my own protocol. The calculator emits po, pe, both marginals and the full confusion matrix, and never a verbal benchmark like “substantial.”

06What the runs exposed

A task card measuring something real that isn't what it claims

One card asked the synthetic user to repair a stale page. Zero of fifteen traces produced the correct content — because those events existed nowhere in the apparatus. The card measures how an attitude handles an impossible repair: interesting, and not what its stated outcome measure assumed. One trace stamped a fresh “last updated” date over unchanged stale content and would have passed any mechanical “did the file change?” test. That single trace is why the apparatus now captures end state and codes correctness instead of trusting a changed-flag.

A confound that invalidated a cell

The cleanest-looking split in that batch tracked terminal use, not mindset. My harness grants the agent no shell tool, and the app under test has no terminal — but the container desktop it runs on has a terminal launcher on its taskbar, and some conditions left the app and clicked it. So those cells cannot carry a grounding claim. I kept the terminal and promoted leaving-the-product to a measure in its own right, prospectively; the fifteen already-run traces stay confounded and labelled, not reinterpreted.

A uniform tax on a differentially scarce budget

All 30 traces in the main batch lost a measured 6–27% of active turns to a clipboard fallback, because the pinned product image shipped no clipboard utility. The tax is uniform across conditions — but it is charged in active turns, the cap binds on active turns, and the censoring is differential. A flat cost paid out of an unequal budget lands hardest on exactly the conditions under study. This is the reasoning that turned “raise the cap” into “stop running.”

A smaller one worth naming: the container locale change that forced an entire run-cut into an archive appears in no trace artifact — not the manifest, not the run config. It survives only in a derived filename and in prose. Recording container environment beside the image digest is a fix I have specified and not yet implemented.

07What I built to make it run

  • Container-per-product apparatus — one frozen product image per arm, pinned by digest, rebuilt per trial. Not a shared VM, which accumulates state across conditions and makes runs non-comparable.
  • A GUI-only capture harness that drives the product through its own interface and writes every stream a measure needs: turn screenshots, think-aloud narration, transcript, token and cost ledger, stop-line and vocabulary detection, run manifest.
  • A comparability proof, not a comparability assumption. The seeded starting artifact is hashed on the host, re-hashed inside the container, and re-hashed again at run start, and every condition's manifest must show the same hash. A run that needs a seed and doesn't get one aborts before spending.
  • One shared source of truth for trial generation, consumed by both the browser tool and the CLI so they cannot drift — verified by generating identical configs for the same selection.
  • Tooling that refuses to guess: the parser stops on an unrecognised condition name and hard-errors on a cross-wave batch; the batch driver front-loads every $0 check so nothing boots or spends on a bad wave.

One side thread: because the project kept calling its subjects “builders,” I embedded 64 role terms with two corpus-trained models against anchor axes declared in the script, and found the candidate terms are not interchangeable — and that a result only one model produces is a property of that model, not of English.

08What I would do differently

  • I would pre-register the turn cap as an outcome class from run one. I treated a cap hit as a failure rather than as a censored observation, and that single framing error has the widest blast radius in the project — it is the reason 30 traces cannot yet support a claim.
  • I would version the environment, not just the image. A container locale change cost me a whole run-cut, and it is recorded nowhere a future reader would look.
  • I would design one task card with a knowable right answer first. Two of the four shared cards I extended could not score correctness at all; I retrofitted the fix after fifteen traces had already been spent on one of them.
  • I would write the rubric for every measure before coding, not just the one I cared most about. The 0.674/0.417 split is the cost of that omission, measured.

All conditions, carriers and traces described here are synthetic. Interview material is confidential: no raw transcripts, no identifiable participant detail, and only de-identified, aggregate summary leaves a study folder. Raw agent traces are local-only and never committed, because screenshots can carry an account identity. Every number on this page comes from calibration runs and none is presented as a counted result.
